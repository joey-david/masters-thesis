\section{Fine-tuning under an information budget}
\label{sec:fineqcomp}

\subsection{Literature review}

\subsubsection{Low-dimensional adaptation}

  Intrinsic-dimension results show that successful language-model adaptation can lie in a much smaller subspace than the full parameter space \parencite{aghajanyan2021intrinsic}. The original LoRA method leverages that constraint, by learning a low-rank update to selected weight matrices \parencite{hu2022lora}. On top of LoRA, AdaLoRA allocates rank by an importance score rather than fixing the same rank throughout the model \parencite{zhang2023adalora}. Work on LoRA factor asymmetry further shows that the input and output factors can contribute unequally despite having the same rank and similar storage cost \parencite{zhu2024asymmetry}.

\subsubsection{Quantization and quantization-aware adaptation}

Whole-model quantization provides several ways to allocate error. GPTQ uses approximate second-order information during sequential weight quantization \parencite{frantar2023gptq}. ZeroQuant uses hardware-aware groups and layer-wise distillation \parencite{yao2022zeroquant}; SmoothQuant moves activation difficulty into weights before applying integer quantization \parencite{xiao2023smoothquant}; and AWQ preserves channels selected from activation statistics \parencite{lin2024awq}. These methods show that equal nominal width does not imply equal functional cost across weights or layers.

QLoRA trains higher-precision adapters over a four-bit frozen backbone \parencite{dettmers2023qlora}. LoftQ chooses an initialization that reduces joint quantization and low-rank approximation error \parencite{li2024loftq}, while QA-LoRA aligns quantization groups with the adapter so that the learned change can merge into a low-bit model \parencite{xu2024qalora}. Their main targets are training memory, initialization error, or low-bit deployment. They do not directly measure the shortest complete file that reproduces the behavior of one fixed trained adapter.

\subsubsection{Description length, bounds, and open measurement}

Rate--distortion theory formalizes the least message rate compatible with a chosen distortion \parencite{berger1971rate,cover2006elements}. Minimum Description Length (MDL) treats short joint descriptions as a model-selection principle \parencite{grunwald2019mdl}. Several more recent results make these ideas concrete in neural networks.

\textcite{young2025radio} takes the rate--distortion view literally in \emph{Radio}, framing post-training LLM quantization as a trade-off between the number of transmitted bits and the distortion induced by compression. Their method can target either a desired compressed model size or a tolerated loss of accuracy. Their framing of compression as an allocation problem under a global rate budget highlights that a bit need not have the same functional value everywhere in a network.

\textcite{liu2025paretoq} give a complementary empirical picture with \emph{ParetoQ}, a unified framework for comparing 1-, 1.58-, 2-, 3-, and 4-bit quantization. They report a marked transition between two and three bits: at three bits and above, fine-tuned quantized models remain comparatively close to the pretrained distribution, whereas at two bits and below the learned representations change much more substantially. Their size--accuracy results also show that lower nominal precision does not translate monotonically into worse trade-offs. This hints at bits per parameter not being a linear measure of usable model capacity; and at the behavioral meaning of a rate depending on representation and training regime.

A different connection between compression and learning appears in the older work of \textcite{arora2018compression}. They construct explicit succinct reparameterizations of trained deep networks and use them to obtain generalization bounds substantially stronger than naive parameter counting. They rely on noise-stability properties, that let the compressed network approximately preserve the original predictions. Later PAC-Bayes work develops related compression-based bounds \parencite{zhou2019pacbayes,lotfi2022pacbayes}.

Finally, \textcite{bergstrasser2026succinct} study description length from the opposite direction in \emph{Transformers are Inherently Succinct}. In a formal-language setting, they prove that fixed-precision transformers can represent some languages exponentially more succinctly than linear temporal logic or recurrent networks, and doubly exponentially more succinctly than finite automata. Though not about fine-tuning, this theorem gives a useful intuition for our setting: the frozen transformer is a powerful decoder rather than passive side information, and a small update may select or specify a behavior whose standalone description would be far larger. This makes the relevant quantity a description \emph{relative to the base model} rather than an intrinsic description of the task.

\subsection{May pilots: deciding which variable to measure}

Throughout this section, a training set is a collection of supervised prompt--response \emph{examples}. We call one model--dataset--training condition an experimental \emph{arm}, and a related collection of arms a \emph{panel}.

Once our main literature review phase was over, we wanted to use a cheap measurable proxy for how much a fine-tune writes. The LoRA's matrices' rank was the cheapest candidate, as if useful adaptation really occupies a small subspace, the smallest effective rank might behave like a crude information budget. We therefore began with a small QLoRA rank sweep before building a true codec.

An initial Qwen2.5-0.5B sweep used instruction-following examples from the \emph{Alpaca} and \emph{Dolly} datasets, with LoRA ranks $\in \{0, 2, 4, 8, 16\}$; rank 0 being the frozen-model baseline. Most improvement in held-out next-token loss appeared between rank 0 and rank 2, with smaller gains thereafter.  Useful adaptation occupied a small low-rank subspace in these runs.

On top of rank, we also wondered whether the amount or ordinary compressibility of the training data predicted how much information would be written. Our pilot dataset variants either duplicated examples or made their prompt--response templates more regular. Exact duplication is deliberately content-free, as it increases the nominal number of stored examples without adding a new training case. But under epoch-based training, it also changes optimization exposure and revisiting frequency.

Separately, $A$-only (with $A$ being the down-projecting matrix of the LoRA adapter) LoRA training stayed near baseline, while $B$-only recovered much of full-LoRA performance in the tested setup. This functional asymmetry warned against treating equally sized parts of the adapter as equally valuable.

Though they carried a lot of negative results and imprecisions, these initial pilots helped us refine our intuition and eliminate a lot of easy targets: nominal rank is not a rate, nominal example count is confounded with training exposure, superficial corpus compressibility is not a clean information intervention, and a small exact-accuracy benchmark is too noisy. Our next methodological step was therefore to hold one learned update fixed and vary only how many bits were used to store it.

\subsection{Fixed-checkpoint exact-rate protocol}

To turn the question into a rate--distortion experiment, we froze a trained adapter and varied only the message used to reconstruct it. This removed retraining as a source of variation and makes every point on the curve a different encoding of the same learned update.

We train one rank-16 LoRA adapter once, then compress (via python's \emph{LZMA}) that same adapter to several file sizes without retraining. Each compressed adapter is serialized to disk, reloaded into the frozen base model, and evaluated. The reported rate is the final file size divided by the number of values in the original adapter, in bits per adapter value.

For rates of one bit and above, we quantize each matrix row independently. At one bit, every value is replaced by either $+\operatorname{mean}|w|$ or $-\operatorname{mean}|w|$ according to its sign; higher-rate variants use more quantization levels. For rates below one bit per value, quantization alone is impossible, so we instead remove matched LoRA rank components, always dropping the corresponding directions in $A$ and $B$ together. Thus the low-rate end of the curve combines rank reduction below one bit with ordinary quantization at one bit and above.

Behavior is measured primarily by held-out next-token negative log-likelihood. If $L_0$ is the frozen base-model loss, $L_{\rm raw}$ the raw-adapter loss, and $L_r$ the loss after decoding a file at rate $r$, we define retained prediction gain as
\[
\operatorname{Retain}(r)=\frac{L_0-L_r}{L_0-L_{\rm raw}}.
\]
The main threshold $\Rstar(.90)$ is the smallest measured serialized rate with at least $90\%$ retained gain, which we recurrently and arbitrarily choose as a threshold for satisfactory performance recovery.

\subsection{Adaptive allocation: a negative result}
\label{subsec:adaptive-allocation}

Once the rate axis was defined, the next interrogation was on how to spend bits could be spent intelligently. Reconstruction error was our instinctive, but willingly naive (as many of the neural-net oriented quantization algorithms exist precisely because RMSE minimization, or even $L_1$ error minimization does not preserve performance satisfyingly) first proxy: if matrix rows whose weights are reconstructed most poorly are also those whose corruption harms behavior most, an MDL-style allocator should beat uniform precision at the same file size.

Our adaptive codec allowed each row of a LoRA factor matrix to be dropped or encoded at 1, 2, 3, 4, or 8 bits. A Lagrangian allocator minimized reconstruction error plus a code-length penalty. The hypothesis was that spending bits on matrix rows with the best error reduction would dominate uniform coding at a matched file rate.

The adaptive codec did not dominate uniform coding. Row dropping could remove a required member of a LoRA pair, while a small global weight error could occur at a behaviorally sensitive location. In the decisive comparison, an adaptive point with lower adapter RMSE lost far more task behavior than a fixed-rate point with slightly higher RMSE.

\subsection{Distinct examples at fixed compute}

A better global norm would not solve the problem if location and downstream use determined the cost of an error. We thus added uniform controls, paired sub-bit selection, and later the layer-band interventions to test those factors.

The RMSE failure showed that weight error alone was a poor guide for allocating bits. More importantly, it shifted the question from \emph{where} to spend a fixed budget to what determines the required budget in the first place.

We first tested whether that budget grows with the amount of distinct training data. A naive dataset-size sweep would also change training exposure, so we instead varied the number of distinct MetaMathQA examples from 2,000 to 32,000 while holding both optimizer steps (2,000) and total example presentations (32,000) fixed, adjusting the number of epochs accordingly. The fitted line in \Cref{fig:distinct-rate} hides part of the evidence. The arms begin to separate near a quarter bit per value and retain their ordering over much of the ladder; $\Rstar$ records one horizontal crossing of that change. The dense sub-bit extension was needed because the original one-to-two-bit sweep placed every crossing below its first measured point.

The distinct-example trend was suggestive but still ambiguous: more distinct examples can also mean more genuinely different underlying content, not just fewer repetitions. To isolate that distinction, an 8,000-example follow-up held the total number of training examples fixed while varying how many source problems generated them. With six seeds and roughly 400, 1,200, 3,600, and 5,620 distinct source problems, $\Rstar$ rose from 0.682 to 0.742 bits per value; the 400 and 3,600 seed ranges did not overlap. This supports content, rather than example count alone, as a cause. Yet pooled math runs also show that much of the change follows the behavioral gain achieved by the raw adapter.

The results initially suggested a simple account: more distinct content required a larger adapter message. Each arm, however, had also learned a different amount. Retaining 90\% of a larger prediction change sets a harder absolute target. We therefore designed the next controls to separate source information, achieved behavior, and task type.

\FloatBarrier
\subsection{Known-information control}

The natural-data trend still did not tell us what ``information'' meant, or whether our proxies for it were even calibrated. We therefore moved to a synthetic mapping task where the amount of source information is known by construction, so the information measure itself could be checked against a known answer before relating it to adapter rate.

Each prompt asks for one of 16 one-token labels. Conditions reuse prototype label tables so the number of independent four-bit draws is known. To estimate description length from prediction, a conditional prequential code trains only on earlier blocks and accumulates the log loss assigned to later blocks. Its preregistered uniform mixture prevents a confidently wrong model from assigning unbounded code length; a later switch-code fix also lets it fall back to the shared base model for a whole block.

The corrected code orders the 4, 64, 128, 256, and 512-bit conditions and lies within 1.1--3.5 times the known count. This validates it as a coarse data-information measure. Adapter $\Rstar$, however, is 0.842, 0.796, 0.663, and 1.013 from 64 through 512 source bits, with roughly $\pm0.3$ bits of seed spread. It does not track source information. The highest-information conditions saturate the finite prompt domain, while the adapter can encode a reusable rule rather than the sampled table. This negative result rules out the claim that dataset entropy alone sets adapter rate.

\begin{figure}[!htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=.98\linewidth]{fineqcomp/plots/known_information_validation.pdf}
\caption[Known source information versus adapter rate.]{Known-information control. The conditional code recovers the correct order of source information after the switch-code repair (left), but adapter $\Rstar(.90)$ does not follow the known bits (right). Error bars show the seed range. The two panels separate validation of the information measure from validation of the proposed rate law.}
\label{fig:known-info}
\end{figure}

The code passed that calibration check, so a broken entropy proxy cannot explain the null $\Rstar$ relation by itself. The adapter can exploit structure shared across mappings. The bits sampled to create a dataset and the bits needed to implement a rule that fits it are different quantities.

\subsection{Searching for model-relative dataset information}
\label{subsec:model-relative-information}

The synthetic null ruled out a simple dataset-entropy law, but it left open a more plausible conditional quantity: information that is new to a particular frozen model. Before constructing such a measure, we tested two simpler explanations---the amount learned and corpus identity---and asked whether changing the receiver changes the ordering.

The first math panel suggested that the size of the learned change might set the budget: across 48 rank-16 runs, held-out bits saved explained $56\%$ of the variation in $\Rstar$. That fit covered only 0.16 bits saved per token. To test whether this relation survived changes in surface form, we rewrote the same target answers in five deterministic styles, from plain narrow responses to symbolic ones. These transforms nearly double the learned change, from 0.555 to 1.062 bits per token, while $\Rstar$ moves only from 0.684 to 0.763. Across all ten transform arms, the slope is 0.12 with $R^2=0.37$, against 1.90 in the original math fit. A 16-fold optimizer-budget sweep on identical content also moves prediction gain by only 0.04 bits per token while $\Rstar$ falls. The amount learned does not set the rate.

Changing the frozen receiver gives a sharper test. Five corpora were trained on both Mistral-7B and Qwen2.5-7B with the same rank, number of training examples, epochs, and codec ladder. Only dialogue remains in the same position when the corpora are ordered from cheapest to most expensive. Instruction tuning moves from the cheapest Mistral arm to the second-most expensive Qwen arm; summarization and math move in the other direction.

To stress-test the apparent link between learning gain and rate outside the original math setting, we then added structured tasks on which fine-tuning could change behavior much more strongly. The clearest counterexample is XBRL tag extraction: Mistral exact match rises from 0.004 to 0.833 and held-out prediction gain reaches 1.747 bits per token, yet its mean $\Rstar$ is 0.371 bits per value, the lowest arm in the campaign. Across 38 pooled task--model arms, prediction gain and $\Rstar$ correlate $-0.37$. This rejects a positive cost law; the negative correlation is descriptive and does not establish an inverse law.

These failures pointed to a different model-relative object: not information intrinsic to the corpus, but the collection of corrections the supervised data requests from the frozen receiver. We screened ten measures from one frozen-model pass over 256 training examples in each of 78 model--dataset--seed cells. They cover target code length, surprise concentration, representation rank, output-residual rank, and random sketches of the target gradients. For a scored token, the output-head gradient factors as
\[
g_{it}=h_{it}\otimes\left(p_{it}-e_{y_{it}}\right),
\]
where $h_{it}$ is the receiver's hidden state, $p_{it}$ its next-token distribution, and $e_{y_{it}}$ the target indicator. Fixed random projections reduce both factors before their outer product. The resulting empirical second moment $F=n^{-1}G^\top G$ describes how many correction directions the dataset requests and how large they are. The main scalar is
\[
I_{\mathrm{corr}}(D;M)=\log\det(I+F)=\sum_j\log(1+\lambda_j(F)).
\]
It is conditional on both dataset $D$ and frozen model $M$: changing either changes the residuals, hidden states, and gradient spectrum.

The reduction first averages the three seeds, then merges four exact data reuses between the high-gain and diversity panels. This leaves 22 distinct model--dataset arms in seven task families. Table~\ref{tab:relative-info-screen} reports the main screens. The permutation test shuffles $\Rstar$ within each model and uses the largest result across all ten candidates, which adjusts for choosing the best measure on this panel. The task-held-out fit leaves one whole family out and uses a model intercept plus one logged measure.

The correction spectrum also recovers the controlled split. Log-volume is nearly flat as SQL groups increase, with slopes $-0.0008$ and $+0.0031$ per doubling on Mistral and Qwen, but rises on XBRL with slopes $+0.0364$ and $+0.0353$. Effective rank retains a positive residual relation after model and task intercepts are removed ($r=0.566$). Its median seed coefficient of variation is 0.045; log-volume's is 0.018. Neither is a renamed learning-gain measure: log-volume has Spearman correlation $-0.275$ with held-out gain across the distinct arms.

This is the first proxy to clear the internal screen, but the panel remains a discovery set. The sketch covers the output-head gradient factor rather than the full LoRA Fisher, the same arms selected and assessed the ten candidates, and the receivers are only two 7B models. The current claim is therefore that a correction-gradient spectrum is a credible model-relative measure of learned-dataset information and predicts the measured thresholds in this panel. A locked prospective model--task panel must test the fixed definition before it becomes a scaling law.

Even a useful task-level predictor could be misleading if the adapter carrier itself imposed a large structural cost. We therefore returned to two substrate controls: how whole-file rate scales with LoRA rank, and whether broad layer location can guide bit allocation. Quadrupling rank from 16 to 64 raises whole-file $\Rstar$ by about threefold, from 28.6 to 82.7 Mbit in the 400-problem arm and from 41.4 to 129.3 Mbit in the 32,000-example arm. This is closer to container scaling than to a fixed task message. At matched 0.5- and 1.0-bit budgets, uniform allocation also beats starving the early, middle, or late third of layers; starving late layers costs most. Weight-update RMS stays nearly flat across depth while representation shift grows toward the last layer. No one band is dispensable in this test.

\FloatBarrier
\subsection{Takeaways}

Exact serialization asks how short a usable learned update can be, rather than how large an adapter was trained. The strongest current claim is conditional: the behavior-preserving bit budget tracks the spectral volume of corrections requested by the learned dataset relative to its frozen receiver. It does not track information intrinsic to the dataset, base loss, or the amount learned alone. Content diversity can shift the threshold at a fixed number of training examples, but the XBRL/SQL split shows that this effect depends on task structure.

Weight RMSE, source entropy, behavioral gain, divergence from the base model, rank, task type, and layer location each expose part of the system; none gives a task-independent law. The correction spectrum is the first tested measure that predicts across task families and follows the SQL/XBRL contrast, but it still needs a prospective test. In the five-corpus Mistral panel, about one bit per adapter value retains essentially all held-out gain, which is the broad practical result in that substrate. Below that rate, the ordering depends strongly on corpus, base model, and the model-relative corrections requested by the data.
