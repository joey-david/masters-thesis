\section{Fine-tuning under an information budget}
\label{sec:fineqcomp}

This chapter asks the training-time version of the question announced in the introduction: can the useful information in a fine-tune be read from the dataset or adapter alone, or does it only become meaningful relative to a frozen receiver and a behavior to preserve? We did not assume the latter at the start. The experiments below show how successive failures of simpler proxies pushed us toward it.

\subsection{Literature review}

\subsubsection{Low-dimensional adaptation}

  Intrinsic-dimension results show that successful language-model adaptation can lie in a much smaller subspace than the full parameter space \parencite{aghajanyan2021intrinsic}. The original LoRA method leverages that constraint, by learning a low-rank update to selected weight matrices \parencite{hu2022lora}. On top of LoRA, AdaLoRA allocates rank by an importance score rather than fixing the same rank throughout the model \parencite{zhang2023adalora}. Work on LoRA factor asymmetry further shows that the input and output factors can contribute unequally despite having the same rank and similar storage cost \parencite{zhu2024asymmetry}.

\subsubsection{Quantization and quantization-aware adaptation}

Whole-model quantization provides several ways to allocate error. GPTQ uses approximate second-order information during sequential weight quantization \parencite{frantar2023gptq}. ZeroQuant uses hardware-aware groups and layer-wise distillation \parencite{yao2022zeroquant}; SmoothQuant moves activation difficulty into weights before applying integer quantization \parencite{xiao2023smoothquant}; and AWQ preserves channels selected from activation statistics \parencite{lin2024awq}. These methods show that equal nominal width does not imply equal functional cost across weights or layers.

QLoRA trains higher-precision adapters over a four-bit frozen backbone \parencite{dettmers2023qlora}. LoftQ chooses an initialization that reduces joint quantization and low-rank approximation error \parencite{li2024loftq}, while QA-LoRA aligns quantization groups with the adapter so that the learned change can merge into a low-bit model \parencite{xu2024qalora}. Their main targets are training memory, initialization error, or low-bit deployment. They do not directly measure the shortest complete file that reproduces the behavior of one fixed trained adapter.

\subsubsection{Description length, bounds, and open measurement}

Rate--distortion theory formalizes the least message rate compatible with a chosen distortion \parencite{berger1971rate,cover2006elements}. Minimum Description Length (MDL) treats short joint descriptions as a model-selection principle \parencite{grunwald2019mdl}. Several more recent results make these ideas concrete in neural networks.

\textcite{young2025radio} takes the rate--distortion view literally in \emph{Radio}, framing post-training LLM quantization as a trade-off between the number of transmitted bits and the distortion induced by compression. Their method can target either a desired compressed model size or a tolerated loss of accuracy. Their framing of compression as an allocation problem under a global rate budget highlights that a bit need not have the same functional value everywhere in a network.

\textcite{liu2025paretoq} give a complementary empirical picture with \emph{ParetoQ}, a unified framework for comparing 1-, 1.58-, 2-, 3-, and 4-bit quantization. They report a marked transition between two and three bits: at three bits and above, fine-tuned quantized models remain comparatively close to the pretrained distribution, whereas at two bits and below the learned representations change much more substantially. Their size--accuracy results also show that lower nominal precision does not translate monotonically into worse trade-offs. This hints at bits per parameter not being a linear measure of usable model capacity; and at the behavioral meaning of a rate depending on representation and training regime.

A different connection between compression and learning appears in the older work of \textcite{arora2018compression}. They construct explicit succinct reparameterizations of trained deep networks and use them to obtain generalization bounds substantially stronger than naive parameter counting. They rely on noise-stability properties, that let the compressed network approximately preserve the original predictions. Later PAC-Bayes work develops related compression-based bounds \parencite{zhou2019pacbayes,lotfi2022pacbayes}.

Finally, \textcite{bergstrasser2026succinct} study description length from the opposite direction in \emph{Transformers are Inherently Succinct}. In a formal-language setting, they prove that fixed-precision transformers can represent some languages exponentially more succinctly than linear temporal logic or recurrent networks, and doubly exponentially more succinctly than finite automata. Though not about fine-tuning, this theorem gives a useful intuition for our setting: the frozen transformer is a powerful decoder rather than passive side information, and a small update may select or specify a behavior whose standalone description would be far larger. This makes the relevant quantity a description \emph{relative to the base model} rather than an intrinsic description of the task.

\subsection{Initial pilots}

Throughout this section, a training set is a collection of supervised prompt--response \emph{examples}. We call one model--dataset--training condition an experimental \emph{arm}, and a related collection of arms a \emph{panel}.

\textbf{Hypothesis.} We first asked whether LoRA rank could serve as a cheap proxy for how much a fine-tune writes. If useful adaptation really occupies a small subspace, the smallest effective rank might behave like a crude information budget. We therefore began with a small QLoRA rank sweep before building a true codec.

\textbf{Test and observation.} An initial Qwen2.5-0.5B sweep used instruction-following examples from the \emph{Alpaca} and \emph{Dolly} datasets, with LoRA ranks $\in \{0, 2, 4, 8, 16\}$; rank 0 was the frozen-model baseline. Most improvement in held-out next-token loss appeared between rank 0 and rank 2, with smaller gains thereafter. Useful adaptation therefore occupied a small low-rank subspace in these runs, but rank itself still counted parameters rather than transmitted information.

\textbf{Competing hypothesis.} We also asked whether the amount or ordinary compressibility of the training data predicted how much information would be written. Our pilot dataset variants either duplicated examples or made their prompt--response templates more regular. Exact duplication is deliberately content-free, as it increases the nominal number of stored examples without adding a new training case. But under epoch-based training, it also changes optimization exposure and revisiting frequency.

\textbf{Additional control.} Separately, $A$-only (with $A$ being the down-projecting matrix of the LoRA adapter) LoRA training stayed near baseline, while $B$-only recovered much of full-LoRA performance in the tested setup. This functional asymmetry warned against treating equally sized parts of the adapter as equally valuable.

\textbf{What we ruled out and next question.} These pilots eliminated several easy interpretations: nominal rank is not a rate, nominal example count is confounded with training exposure, superficial corpus compressibility is not a clean information intervention, and a small exact-accuracy benchmark is too noisy. We therefore stopped asking which proxy looked most like information and instead measured an actual message: hold one learned update fixed and vary only how many bits are used to store it.

\subsection{Fixed-checkpoint exact-rate protocol}

To measure an actual rate--distortion curve, we train one rank-16 LoRA adapter and then keep that checkpoint fixed. Every point on the curve is therefore a different compressed representation of the same learned update, rather than a separately trained model.

Our codec quantizes the LoRA factors, bit-packs the quantized values together with their scales and metadata, compresses the payload with zlib, and writes the result to disk. We then reload that file into the frozen base model before evaluation. The reported rate is the complete serialized file size divided by the number of values in the original adapter, in bits per adapter value.

At one bit and above, each LoRA matrix is quantized row by row. At one bit, a value is represented only by its sign and its row scale, so it reconstructs to $+\operatorname{mean}|w|$ or $-\operatorname{mean}|w|$. Higher rates use more quantization levels. Below one bit per value, we cannot give every value even one bit, so we instead keep only a fraction of LoRA rank directions, always keeping or dropping the corresponding directions in $A$ and $B$ together.

We measure behavior mainly with held-out next-token negative log-likelihood. If $L_0$ is the frozen-model loss, $L_{\rm raw}$ the loss of the uncompressed adapter, and $L_r$ the loss after compression to rate $r$, retained gain is
\[
\operatorname{Retain}(r)=\frac{L_0-L_r}{L_0-L_{\rm raw}}.
\]
We use $\Rstar(.90)$ for the smallest measured rate that retains at least 90\% of the uncompressed adapter's gain. The 90\% threshold is simply a consistent operating point for comparing experiments; the full rate--retention curves remain available when the threshold hides useful detail.

\subsection{Adaptive allocation: a negative result}
\label{subsec:adaptive-allocation}

\textbf{Hypothesis.} Once the rate axis was defined, we asked whether a fixed bit budget could be allocated more intelligently than uniform precision. Weight reconstruction error was a deliberately simple first proxy: if the rows that are hardest to reconstruct are also the rows whose corruption matters most for behavior, an error-aware allocator should win at the same file size.

\textbf{Experiment.} The adaptive codec could drop each row of a LoRA factor matrix or encode it at 1, 2, 3, 4, or 8 bits. A Lagrangian objective traded reconstruction error against code length and chose the allocation.

\textbf{Observation.} Contrary to the hypothesis, this did not beat uniform coding. In the clearest counterexample, an adaptive point had lower adapter RMSE than a fixed-rate point but preserved far less of the learned behavior.

\textbf{Interpretation and next question.} This ruled out global weight error as the quantity that orders useful compression. More importantly, it shifted the question from \emph{where} to spend a fixed budget to what determines how large the required budget is in the first place.

\textbf{Alternative explanation and control.} Distinct examples are only a rough proxy for distinct content: two records can express the same underlying problem. We therefore ran a second experiment with exactly 8,000 training examples in every condition, but varied how many source math problems those examples came from. Across six seeds, increasing the number of source problems from roughly 400 to 5,620 raised mean $\Rstar$ from 0.682 to 0.742 bits per value.

\textbf{What remained unresolved.} This made content diversity look more promising than example count alone, but the adapters trained on more diverse data also tended to improve the model more. Retaining 90\% of a larger improvement may itself require a larger file. The next question was therefore whether source information still predicts rate once the information measure itself is calibrated, and whether learning gain can explain the remaining variation.

\FloatBarrier
\subsection{Known-information control}

\textbf{Hypothesis.} If source information itself sets adapter rate, then increasing a known number of source bits should increase $\Rstar$. Natural corpora do not give us a ground-truth information count, so we built a synthetic mapping task where that count is known by construction. Each prompt maps to one of 16 one-token labels, and the conditions differ in the number of independent four-bit label assignments used to generate the data.

\textbf{Experiment.} We estimated the dataset's description length with a conditional \emph{prequential} code: the model is trained on earlier blocks and used to encode later ones through their log loss. A uniform fallback prevents a confidently wrong model from assigning an arbitrarily large code length, and a later switch-code correction lets an entire block fall back to the shared base model when that is cheaper.

\textbf{Observation.} After that correction, the prequential code orders the 4-, 64-, 128-, 256-, and 512-bit conditions correctly and stays within 1.1--3.5 times the known source count, so the information measure is at least directionally calibrated. Adapter $\Rstar$, however, does not follow that ordering: across the 64-, 128-, 256-, and 512-bit conditions its mean values are 0.842, 0.796, 0.663, and 1.013 bits per value, with strongly overlapping seed ranges.

\begin{figure}[!htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=.6\linewidth]{fineqcomp/plots/known_information_validation.pdf}
\caption[Known source information control.]{Known-information control. The conditional code recovers the correct order of source information.}
\label{fig:known-info}
\end{figure}

\textbf{What this ruled out and next question.} The source can therefore contain more sampled information without forcing a proportionally larger behavior-preserving adapter. The adapter may instead pay for a reusable rule relative to what the frozen model already knows. This pushed us from an intrinsic dataset-information hypothesis toward a receiver-relative one.

\FloatBarrier
\subsection{Searching for model-relative dataset information}
\label{subsec:model-relative-information}

\textbf{New hypothesis.} In alignment with the original internship proposal, the synthetic result suggested that the relevant information might be conditional on what the frozen model already ``knows''. Before introducing a new model-relative measure, however, we tested two simpler explanations: perhaps the rate is just determined by how much the fine-tune improves the model, or perhaps each dataset simply has an intrinsic difficulty.

\textbf{Alternative 1: amount learned.} The first math experiments seemed to support the first idea: across 48 rank-16 runs, held-out prediction gain explained 56\% of the variation in $\Rstar$. But that relationship covered only a narrow range of gains. We therefore rewrote the same target answers in five deterministic styles, ranging from short plain responses to symbolic ones. These rewrites nearly doubled the learned prediction gain, from 0.555 to 1.062 bits per token, while $\Rstar$ moved only from 0.684 to 0.763. A separate 16-fold optimizer-budget sweep changed the amount learned with the content fixed and also failed to produce the expected rate increase. The size of the behavioral improvement is therefore not enough to determine the required rate.

\textbf{Receiver test.} We next changed the frozen model while keeping the fine-tuning setup fixed. The same five corpora were trained on Mistral-7B and Qwen2.5-7B with the same LoRA rank, number of examples, epochs, and codec. If a corpus had an intrinsic adapter cost, its ordering should remain similar across the two receivers. It did not: only dialogue kept the same position, while instruction tuning, summarization, and math changed substantially in relative cost.

The XBRL intervention is not a clean measure of abstract diversity: increasing the number of companies also changes which companies the adapter sees during training, while the test set covers all companies. The result may therefore partly reflect support coverage. Nearly constant answer entropy rules out a simple label-entropy explanation, but a cleaner diversity intervention is still needed, and one of the things left on our to-do list at the time of writing.

\textbf{Candidate measure.} By then, the most plausible remaining quantity was the set of corrections that the dataset asks a particular frozen model to make, so we tried to quantify it. Our most useful family of measures came from the token gradients. For a scored token, the output-head gradient factorizes as
\[
g_{it}=h_{it}\otimes\left(p_{it}-e_{y_{it}}\right),
\]
where $h_{it}$ is the hidden state, $p_{it}$ the model's predicted distribution, and $e_{y_{it}}$ the correct target. Intuitively, one factor describes the representation the model is using and the other describes how its prediction needs to change.

We project these factors to a manageable dimension and summarize the spectrum of their empirical second moment $F=n^{-1}G^\top G$. Our main scalar is
\[
I_{\mathrm{corr}}(D;M)=\log\det(I+F)=\sum_j\log(1+\lambda_j(F)).
\]
This quantity grows when the requested corrections are large and spread over many independent directions. Crucially, it depends on both the dataset $D$ and the frozen model $M$: changing the receiver changes its hidden states, its errors, and therefore the correction spectrum.

After averaging seeds and removing exact data reuses, our comparison contains 22 distinct model--dataset conditions across seven task families. We evaluate each candidate both by how well it orders $\Rstar$ within a model and by how well it predicts a task family left out of fitting. Because ten measures were screened on the same data, our reported permutation test also corrected for selecting the best candidate from that set.

The correction spectrum also follows the earlier SQL/XBRL contrast: its log-volume is nearly flat as SQL diversity increases but rises with XBRL diversity on both base models. It is not simply another name for learning gain; across the distinct conditions, correction log-volume and held-out gain have Spearman correlation $-0.275$.

\textbf{Current interpretation.} This is our strongest current predictor. It uses a projected output-head gradient rather than the full LoRA Fisher, and the study contains only two 7B receivers. We therefore treat the correction spectrum as a promising model-relative hypothesis, not yet as an established scaling law. The important conceptual change is already visible, however: useful update information appears to be a property of a dataset--receiver pair under a stated behavioral target, not of the dataset alone.

\subsection{Container and location controls}

\textbf{Substrate control.} Finally, we checked whether the adapter architecture itself imposes a large cost independent of task content. Quadrupling LoRA rank from 16 to 64 increases the whole-file $\Rstar$ by roughly threefold in two matched conditions, suggesting that a substantial part of the message scales with the carrier. We also asked whether broad layer location could guide bit allocation. At matched 0.5- and 1.0-bit budgets, uniform precision beats starving the early, middle, or late third of the network; starving late layers hurts most. Weight-update RMS is almost flat across depth, while the induced representation shift grows toward the final layers.

\FloatBarrier
\subsection{Takeaways}

The main methodological result of this chapter, which tackles the information theoretic limits of finetuning before its merging with reasoning topics in \Cref{sec:unification} is straightforward: if we want to ask how much information a fine-tune writes, we should measure the size of an actual reloadable update that preserves a stated behavior, rather than infer it from LoRA rank or weight error.

The experiments then rule out several simple predictors of that rate. It is not set by source entropy, base-model loss, or the magnitude of the behavioral improvement alone. The same corpus can require different rates on different frozen models, and content diversity matters in some tasks but not others. The strongest current explanation is model-relative: the rate tracks the number and magnitude of correction directions that the training data asks the frozen model to make. That result is promising but still needs prospective replication.
