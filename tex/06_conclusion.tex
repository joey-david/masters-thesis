\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

The internship began with a broad question about information limits in fine-tuning. The word ``information'' proved too loose for a direct test. We replaced each informal term with an object that could fail one. Adapter capacity became an exact decoded file rate; dataset information became a known source count or conditional code. A thought unit became a boundary chosen under a named utility. State became a contract tested for sufficiency, shared meaning, and closure.

That change in language records how the research developed. We began by asking for a limit and found that the terms in the question were not yet measurable. Each phase corrected the measurement used in the one before it. The experiments all ask what ``information'' and ``state'' must mean if they are to explain behavior.

Several hypotheses failed. Adaptive weight-error allocation did not beat fixed coding, and lower RMSE did not ensure better behavior. Known source information did not predict adapter $\Rstar$ by itself. Response transforms and optimizer-budget controls rejected the amount learned as a sufficient predictor, while the ordering of five corpora changed between frozen base models. Native reasoning traces yielded no single partition that served answer, symbolic, correctness, and compression goals. A decodable state did not guarantee one-pass routing. Local transition accuracy of about 89\% still collapsed over 32 recursive register steps.

The failed tests narrowed the claims and set the next experiment. The adaptive-codec result prompted behavioral and location controls. The non-dominant thought-unit result prompted tests of explicit causal state. The register failure separated local skill from recursive reliability.

The surviving claims are narrower. The smallest adapter file that preserves behavior tracks the spectral volume of corrections requested by the learned dataset relative to the frozen receiver. Across 22 distinct two-model arms, correction Fisher log-volume gives mean within-model Spearman correlation 0.764 and lowers task-held-out prediction error by 29\% against a model-only fit; base code length does not. This is a discovery-panel result, not yet a law. Content diversity shifts the threshold at fixed rows on MetaMathQA and XBRL, but not reliably on SQL. Objective-specific reasoning structure is learnable even when no partition dominates. Exact controllers show why initial information must be evaluated through the representation's real update rule. With enough state capacity and closure, a canonical four-bit proof interface remains exact through long padded horizons. Lossy and path-aliased controls isolate separate causes of failure.

An information claim needs a real code, a receiver, stated side information, and a downstream utility. A state claim also needs the update rule that will consume the state again. Without those parts, a small container, good reconstruction score, or strong probe may describe a representation, but it cannot establish that the representation preserves behavior.

The next studies should measure information through the behavior it must preserve and the dynamics it must support. They should vary content diversity while matching training and held-out group support, match absolute learned gain across frozen models, compress one reasoning fine-tune while tracking outputs and internal structure, and predict long-horizon failure from self-conditioned transition errors on state spaces too large to tabulate.

The evidence supports exact update-rate measurement, model-relative learned-dataset information as a screened explanation, objective-relative trajectory analysis, and closed state channels under controlled conditions. It does not support a single law. Any stronger theory must reproduce the correction-spectrum result on new models and tasks, explain the successful proof interface alongside the failed register interface, and explain the XBRL diversity slope alongside the SQL null. One discovery panel cannot resolve those pairs of results.
